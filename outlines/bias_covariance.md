# Sampling bias of linear estimators as a covariance

We show that the *expected* sampling bias of a linear estimator of a domain-mean
anomaly can be written as a covariance between a **sampling weight** and the
**anomaly field**, and we work this out explicitly for the simple-average estimator
and for the (simple-)kriging estimator. The sampling measure is a *random* measure
(the design is random), so the covariance form is a statement about the expected
bias; the per-realization bias is exact in a measure-difference form that needs no
density.

---

## 1. Setup and notation

Let $Z$ be the (fixed) anomaly field on a spatiotemporal domain $\mathcal{X}$.

The observed profiles are generated by a **random sampling design**: an outcome
$\omega$ in a probability space $(\Omega, \mathbb{P}_{\text{design}})$ produces
profile locations $x_1(\omega),\ldots,x_{n(\omega)}(\omega)$ (e.g. from advection and
deployment). Quantities built from the profiles are therefore random through
$\omega$; we suppress $\omega$ where it is clear.

A **linear predictor** at a prediction spacetime point $x^*$ has the form, for a
given realization,

$$
\hat Z(x^*) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, Z(x_i).
$$

Any such predictor induces a **random empirical sampling measure** on $\mathcal{X}$,

$$
\mathbb{Q} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, \delta_{x_i},
$$

so that the estimator is an integral of the field against $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}} \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z(x)\, d\mathbb{Q}(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, Z(x_i).
$$

Both $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}}$ are random (they depend on $\omega$).

Let $\mathbb{P}$ be the target measure: the normalized area–time measure over the
domain. The **estimand** is the (deterministic) domain-mean anomaly

$$
\overline{\mathrm{OHC}} \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z(x)\, d\mathbb{P}(x),
$$

and the **bias** is the random quantity

$$
B \;=\; \widehat{\mathrm{OHC}} - \overline{\mathrm{OHC}}.
$$

For the simple-average estimator, $a_i = 1/n$ for all $i$.

---

## 2. The expected sampling measure and the sampling weight

Because $\mathbb{Q}$ is a random measure, its natural summary is its expectation over
the design. We define the **expected sampling measure** $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]$ through
its action on test functions: $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]$ is the measure for which, for
every bounded measurable $f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$,

$$
\int_{\mathcal{X}} f\, d\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]
\;:=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}} f\, d\mathbb{Q}\right]
\;=\;
\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}} f(x)\, d\mathbb{Q}^{\omega}(x)\right) d\mathbb{P}_{\text{design}}(\omega)
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\, f(x_i)\right].
\tag{2.1}
$$

This is the **mean (first-moment) measure**, or intensity measure, of the random
measure $\mathbb{Q}$; equivalently $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}](A) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}(A)]$
for every measurable set $A$ (take $f=\mathbb{1}_A$).

We then define the **sampling weight** as the density of the expected sampling
measure with respect to the target,

$$
w \;=\; \frac{d\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]}{d\mathbb{P}},
$$

a Radon–Nikodym derivative: a $\mathbb{P}$-almost-everywhere-defined measurable
function, existing when $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}] \ll \mathbb{P}$. It is the normalized
**expected sampling intensity**: $w(x)>1$ where the design over-weights the domain
in expectation relative to its true area share, $w(x)<1$ where it under-weights.

> **Remark (why the expectation is needed).** A single realized measure
> $\mathbb{Q}^{\omega}=\sum_i a_i\delta_{x_i}$ is purely atomic, while $\mathbb{P}$ is
> non-atomic; the two are mutually singular, so the realized measure has **no**
> density with respect to $\mathbb{P}$ and $d\mathbb{Q}^{\omega}/d\mathbb{P}$ does not
> exist as a function. Averaging over the design smears the atoms across $\mathcal{X}$:
> if the profile locations have a continuous distribution under
> $\mathbb{P}_{\text{design}}$, then $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}] \ll \mathbb{P}$ and $w$ is a
> genuine $L^1(\mathbb{P})$ function. This is why the sampling weight, and the
> covariance form below, are statements about the **expected** measure.

---

## 3. Bias identity: realized and expected

**Realized bias (exact, random, no density needed).** For any single design outcome,
both estimator and estimand integrate the *same* field $Z$, so the realized bias is
the field integrated against the difference of the two measures:

$$
B \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{Q} \;-\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{P}
  \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d(\mathbb{Q}-\mathbb{P}).
\tag{3.1}
$$

This holds exactly for the atomic realized measure and requires no absolute
continuity.

**Expected bias (density form).** Take the expectation over the design and use the
test-function definition (2.1) with $f = Z$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[B]
\;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{Q}\right] - \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{P}
\;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}] - \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{P}.
$$

Writing $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]$ through its density $w=d\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]/d\mathbb{P}$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[B]
\;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, w\, d\mathbb{P} - \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{P}
\;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\,(w-1)\, d\mathbb{P}.
\tag{3.2}
$$

The covariance identity in the next section is thus a statement about $\mathbb{E}[B]$,
the bias averaged over the sampling design.

---

## 4. Expected bias as a covariance

Define the **expected total mass** of the sampling measure,

$$
m \;=\; \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}](\mathcal{X}) \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} w\, d\mathbb{P}
  \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\right].
\tag{4.1}
$$

The covariance between the sampling weight $w$ and the field $Z$ under $\mathbb{P}$ is,
by definition (both $w$ and $Z$ are now deterministic functions on $\mathcal{X}$),

$$
\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z)
  \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} w\, Z\, d\mathbb{P}
      \;-\; \Big(\int_{\mathcal{X}} w\, d\mathbb{P}\Big)\Big(\int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\mathbb{P}\Big).
\tag{4.2}
$$

Identify the two integrals. The first is the expected estimator, by (2.1) with $f=Z$:

$$
\int_{\mathcal{X}} w\, Z\, d\mathbb{P} \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}}\big].
$$

The second factors as $m\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}$. Hence

$$
\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}}\big] \;-\; m\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}.
\tag{4.3}
$$

Rearranging $\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}}] = \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w,Z) + m\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}$
and subtracting the estimand gives the **expected-bias-as-covariance identity**

$$
\boxed{\;
\mathbb{E}[B] \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z) \;+\; (m-1)\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}
\;}
\tag{4.4}
$$

The first term is the **alignment** of the expected sampling weight with the field
(the extent to which the design over-weights the domain, in expectation, exactly
where the anomaly is large). The second term, $(m-1)\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}$, is a
**mass-deficit / shrinkage** term: it vanishes when the estimator conserves mass in
expectation ($m=1$) and is otherwise proportional to the estimand.

**Unit-mass case.** If $m=1$ the shrinkage term drops and the expected bias is a pure
covariance,

$$
m = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[B] \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z).
\tag{4.5}
$$

## 5. Simple-average estimator

For the simple average, every observation gets equal weight,

$$
a_i \;=\; \frac{1}{n}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n,
$$

so, for each realization, the induced sampling measure is the empirical (occupancy)
measure

$$
\mathbb{Q} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{x_i},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Z(x_i).
$$

**Mass.** For *every* realization $\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \tfrac{1}{n} = 1$
(regardless of the random $n$), so the expected mass is

$$
m \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_i a_i\right] \;=\; 1.
$$

The simple average conserves mass deterministically. Its sampling weight $w =
d\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]/d\mathbb{P}$ is the normalized **expected occupancy intensity**
(expected profiles per unit area–time, normalized), with $\int w\,d\mathbb{P}=1$.

**Expected bias.** By (4.5) the shrinkage term vanishes and the expected bias is
exactly a covariance:

$$
\boxed{\;
\mathbb{E}[B_{\text{avg}}] \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z)
\;}
\tag{5.1}
$$

The simple-average bias is, in expectation, the covariance between how the array
over/under-samples the domain and the anomaly field. It is zero whenever the expected
occupancy is uncorrelated with the field — even if the occupancy is strongly
non-uniform.

## 6. Kriging estimator

### 6.1 Point-prediction weights

The (simple-)kriging predictor at a prediction point $x^*$ is linear in the
observations with weights determined by the covariance model:

$$
\hat Z(x^*) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i(x^*)\, Z(x_i),
\qquad
\alpha_i(x^*) \;=\; \Big[\, \mathbf{k}^*(x^*)^\top \big(K + \sigma^2 I\big)^{-1} \Big]_i,
\tag{6.1}
$$

where $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$ is the covariance among observations, $\sigma^2$ is the
nugget, and $\mathbf{k}^*(x^*)_i = k(x^*, x_i)$ are the target-to-observation
covariances. The weights $\alpha_i(x^*)$ are random through the design (they depend on
the profile locations, and on any fitted covariance parameters), but for a given
realization they are fixed numbers.

### 6.2 The estimand fixes the measure: aggregate over the prediction grid

The domain-mean anomaly is estimated by predicting on a grid of locations
$\{x^*_g\}_{g=1}^G$ and averaging with area weights $c_g$, where $\sum_g c_g = 1$:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}} \;=\; \sum_{g=1}^G c_g\, \hat Z(x^*_g)
                     \;=\; \sum_{g=1}^G c_g \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i(x^*_g)\, Z(x_i).
$$

Interchange the finite sums:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}}
\;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \Big( \underbrace{\sum_{g=1}^G c_g\, \alpha_i(x^*_g)}_{\displaystyle a_i} \Big) Z(x_i)
\;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, Z(x_i),
\tag{6.2}
$$

which defines the **aggregated weights**

$$
\boxed{\;
a_i \;=\; \sum_{g=1}^G c_g\, \alpha_i(x^*_g)
\;}
\tag{6.3}
$$

Each observation's weight is the area-weighted sum, over all prediction points, of
that point's kriging weight on it. The induced (random) sampling measure for the
estimand is

$$
\mathbb{Q} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\, \delta_{x_i} \;=\; \sum_{g=1}^G c_g\, \mathbb{Q}_{x^*_g},
\qquad \mathbb{Q}_{x^*} := \sum_i \alpha_i(x^*)\,\delta_{x_i},
$$

i.e. the family of point measures averaged against the prediction area weights. With
$\mathbb{Q}$ in hand, kriging is back in the general form
$\widehat{\mathrm{OHC}} = \int Z\, d\mathbb{Q}$, and its sampling weight is
$w = d\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]/d\mathbb{P}$ as in Section 2.

### 6.3 Expected mass of the kriging measure

Define the per-prediction-point weight-sum

$$
s_g \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i(x^*_g).
$$

The realized mass is $\sum_i a_i = \sum_g c_g s_g$, and the expected mass (4.1) is

$$
m \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\right] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{g=1}^G c_g\, s_g\right].
\tag{6.4}
$$

For **simple kriging** the weights are not constrained to sum to one (there is no
unbiasedness/Lagrange constraint); the predictor blends the data with the prior
mean, which here is zero because $Z$ is the mean-subtracted anomaly. As a prediction
point moves away from the data (relative to the fitted decorrelation scale), the
covariances $\mathbf{k}^*(x^*)\to \mathbf{0}$, the weights shrink, and

$$
s_g \;=\; \sum_i \alpha_i(x^*_g) \;\le\; 1,
\qquad s_g \to 0 \ \text{far from data.}
$$

Hence $m = \mathbb{E}[\sum_g c_g s_g] \le 1$, with equality only if every prediction
point is fully informed ($s_g \equiv 1$, e.g. ordinary kriging, or dense coverage
everywhere). The quantity $1-s_g$ is the fraction of prediction point $g$'s weight
that reverts to the mean, and $1-m = \mathbb{E}[\sum_g c_g(1-s_g)]$ is the expected
**mean-reversion mass** of the estimator.

### 6.4 Expected kriging bias as a covariance plus shrinkage

Apply the general identity (4.4) with the kriging expected mass (6.4):

$$
\boxed{\;
\mathbb{E}[B_{\text{krig}}] \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z) \;+\; (m-1)\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}},
\qquad m = \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\textstyle\sum_g c_g\, s_g\Big] \le 1
\;}
\tag{6.5}
$$

with $w = d\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]/d\mathbb{P}$ the sampling weight of the aggregated
kriging measure. The two terms:

- **Alignment**, $\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z)$: the extent to which the
  expected kriging-weighted sampling pattern correlates with the anomaly field. The
  $K^{-1}$ in (6.1) declusters redundant observations, so $w$ is closer to uniform
  than the raw occupancy, but any residual correlation with $Z$ biases the estimate.

- **Shrinkage**, $(m-1)\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}$: because simple kriging reverts to the
  (zero) mean where it is under-informed, it withholds a fraction $1-m$ of the total
  weight in expectation, pulling the domain estimate toward zero in proportion to the
  estimand. This term is **directional**: for a warm year ($\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}>0$)
  it biases the estimate low; for a cold year it biases high. It is the
  regression-toward-climatology of an under-covering array.

### 6.5 Special cases

- **Ordinary kriging / full coverage.** If $s_g \equiv 1$ then $m=1$, the shrinkage
  term vanishes, and $\mathbb{E}[B_{\text{krig}}] = \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z)$,
  the same pure-covariance form as the simple average (5.1); only the weight $w$
  differs (declustered kriging weights rather than raw occupancy).

- **Simple average as a special case.** Taking $\alpha_i(x^*)\equiv 1/n$ gives
  $a_i = \sum_g c_g/n = 1/n$, $m=1$, and recovers Section 5.

---

## 7. Summary

The sampling measure $\mathbb{Q}=\sum_i a_i\delta_{x_i}$ is random through the design.
Its expectation, defined via test functions by
$\int f\, d\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}] = \mathbb{E}[\sum_i a_i f(x_i)]$, has density
$w = d\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{Q}]/d\mathbb{P}$ (the expected sampling intensity), and
expected mass $m = \mathbb{E}[\sum_i a_i]$.

- **Realized bias** (exact, random, no density):
$$
B \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\, d(\mathbb{Q}-\mathbb{P}).
$$

- **Expected bias** (covariance form):
$$
\mathbb{E}[B] \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} Z\,(w-1)\, d\mathbb{P}
  \;=\; \operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w, Z) + (m-1)\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}.
$$

| Estimator | weights | expected mass $m$ | expected bias $\mathbb{E}[B]$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple average | $a_i = 1/n$ | $1$ | $\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w,Z)$ |
| Ordinary kriging | $a_i=\sum_g c_g\,\alpha_i(x^*_g)$, $s_g\equiv1$ | $1$ | $\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w,Z)$ |
| Simple kriging | $a_i=\sum_g c_g\,\alpha_i(x^*_g)$, $s_g\le1$ | $\mathbb{E}[\sum_g c_g s_g] \le 1$ | $\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(w,Z) + (m-1)\,\overline{\mathrm{OHC}}$ |

The simple-average and ordinary-kriging expected biases are pure alignment
covariances between the expected sampling weight and the field. Simple kriging adds
one scalar shrinkage term, proportional to the estimand, arising from its
non-conservation of mass ($m\le 1$) as it reverts to the mean in under-informed
regions.
